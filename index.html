About

Instructional design energizes me — because I've seen what happens when learning experiences have an actual impact.

I've watched as a participant who'd never touched a smartphone in her entire life learned to build websites from scratch. She went on to become a member of the leadership team at a major nonprofit and a published author featured in national media. I've seen adults paralyzed by learning anxiety earn their own "light bulb" moment when everything clicks. In instructional design, success doesn't just feel like a win; it's a reminder about why this work matters.

Over five years in higher education, workforce development, upskilling, and technical training, I've specialized in creating learning experiences for people navigating real barriers. Whether that's educational gaps, life circumstances, systemic inequity, or the all-too-common beast that is learning anxiety. My approach is grounded in adult learning theory, Universal Design for Learning, and the belief that effective instruction treats learners as capable, complex people deserving of thoughtful, equity-centered, and agency-focused design.

I can take overwhelming content or an undefined instructional vision and turn it into something coherent, accessible, and genuinely transformative. I design curriculum that's structured to hold the bar high, but flexible enough to meet learners where they are, so they can lift themselves to that high bar on their own. Whether I'm building a three-month interdisciplinary program from scratch or refreshing existing corporate training, my goal is always the same: help people build skills, confidence, and a sense of what's possible.

📚 Case Study: Building L4L (Leadership for Life)

Program Architecture Trauma-Informed Design Full-Time Learning Ecosystem

L4L was a 12-week, full-time (40 hrs/week) foundational program for formerly incarcerated adults entering a year-long tech career pathway. I designed the entire instructional ecosystem for Pillar 1 — daily pacing, outcomes, assessments, trauma-informed frameworks, SME alignment, and cross-Pillar architecture — and built the emotional, cognitive, and digital foundations learners needed to succeed.

Expand each section below to read the full case study directly on this page.

1. Context: The “We Have a Dream” Phase

CROP hired me at a moment when they had:

  • a three-day seminar
  • extremely passionate goals
  • a full-time year-long “Pillar program” sketched mostly in spirit
  • no actual curriculum (outside of the three-day seminar)

They essentially said,
“We want to teach leadership, digital literacy, financial wellness, mindset, professional skills, reading, writing, sales, and UX Design — all of it — to formerly incarcerated adults trying to rebuild their lives.”

I said,
“Okay… so basically you want to teach DREAMS right now?”

Laughter was exchanged. Then I built it anyway.

SMEs were brought in for Pillars 2, 3, and 4. I led the development of Pillar 1, and oversaw the management of educational cohesion and instructional design across the entire year-long program for all Pillars.

This was, objectively, a senior-level scope. I was fresh enough not to know that — which may be why it worked: I didn’t know it was supposed to be impossible.

And for what it's worth: I can never again hear “We’re building the plane as we fly it” without thinking of the founders who trusted me to figure out how to build their plane.

2. Translating Vision Into Structure

My first job was taking a big, glowing cloud of aspiration and turning it into:

  • concrete learning outcomes
  • a real sequence
  • an actual instructional plan that human beings could complete without collapsing under cognitive load

This meant:

  • identifying the through-line of the entire year-long program
  • defining the purpose of Pillar 1
  • deciding what success looks like at the 12-week mark
  • carving the mountain of content into climbable hills

L4L needed to be the foundation where learners could build stability, skill, and confidence.

3. Learners: Adults With Complex Lives and Incredible Potential

My learners were adults reentering society after incarceration who often faced:

  • disrupted or incomplete formal education
  • limited digital literacy
  • unstable housing or employment
  • trauma histories
  • extremely practical and emotional needs

So the curriculum had to be:

  • trauma-informed
  • confidence-building
  • flexible
  • low-barrier with a high-bar
  • immediately applicable
  • human-centered rather than content-centered

This is why I structured everything around small wins and learner agency.

4. Program Architecture: Does Anybody Know How to Fly This Thing?

L4L wasn’t a “once-a-week” program. It was a full-time, immersive learning environment: 9am to 4pm, five days a week, for three months.

That meant I wasn’t creating a set of lessons — I was building an entire daily learning ecosystem that could hold real humans, with real pasts, for 40 hours a week, without breaking them or burning them out.

The structure had to be:

  • digestible
  • consistent
  • scaffolded
  • emotionally safe
  • cognitively sustainable
  • something instructors could realistically facilitate
  • something learners could survive, grow inside, and not collapse under

The 12-week build emerged from what learners needed and what the full-time format demanded. Instead of treating each topic as discrete, I designed L4L as a three-part instructional architecture, each part reinforcing the others and supporting the daily schedule.

A. Digital Literacy (Foundational Competence)
“Let’s get your hands on the tools and build confidence.”

At 40 hours a week, tech exposure wasn’t a one-off activity — it needed to be built into the daily rhythm so that comfort accumulated naturally.

Daily examples:

  • guided device setup and digital safety practice
  • multi-hour hands-on productivity labs (Docs, Drive, Sheets, file structure)
  • micro-tasks designed for low-stakes repetition throughout the day

Digital Literacy had to go first because learners needed tech fluency to participate in the other Pillars — and because confidence with tools stabilizes emotional bandwidth for everything else.

B. Financial Wellness (Practical Stability)
“Let’s demystify money and build agency.”

At full-time intensity, financial content couldn’t be overwhelming or shame-inducing. It had to be paced, revisited, and integrated into daily structure.

Daily examples:

  • iterative budgeting work (learn → apply → refine)
  • values-based conversations embedded as warm-ups or cool-downs
  • scenario activities that mirrored real-life decision points

Financial literacy grounded learners in real-world stability, giving them clarity and agency in an otherwise high-cognitive-load environment.

C. Leadership Development (The Core of the Program)
“Let’s turn knowledge, mindset, and skills into self-direction.”

This part carried the emotional center of the program. It also carried the most hours.

Leadership Development included:

  • Professional communication → writing and rewriting emails, tone calibration
  • Mindset + motivation → task management, resilience, habit formation
  • Reading + writing practice → discussions, summaries, expressive writing
  • Life-navigation strategies → decision-making, time management, values alignment
  • Agency + radical accountability → learning to hold oneself as responsible for their own choices without collapsing

Because this content was emotionally heavy, I had to design daily pacing that pushed learners without allowing them to break. On a full-time schedule, this meant building a rhythm that alternated between cognitive load, emotional depth, structured practice, and recovery time.

Daily examples:

  • 90-minute mindset workshops with follow-up reflection periods
  • professional communication practice embedded between heavier sessions
  • reading and writing blocks designed to build stamina without overwhelm
  • leadership circles that balanced vulnerability with safety

This category got the most hours because it built both job readiness and personal autonomy — and because learners needed a steady, containered environment for this kind of growth.

System-Level Architecture (What Held the Whole Program Together)

Designing 12 weeks of full-time instruction meant designing the entire infrastructure around the content:

  • rubrics for consistent evaluation across instructors and days
  • daily instructor guides with pacing, timing, scripts, trauma-informed notes, and troubleshooting
  • performance + formative assessments embedded in the weekly structure
  • trauma-informed design baked into lesson flow, timing, activity choice, and facilitation language
  • learner support systems to stabilize navigation through full-time intensity
  • integration of accessible tools (Google Classroom, Digital Skills courses, etc.) to maintain continuity
  • badge-based microcredentials that gave daily and weekly wins
  • learner analytics to catch disengagement before it escalated
  • scenario-based communication practice integrated throughout the week
  • asynchronous modules for differentiated pacing and makeup learning

Cross-Pillar alignment was also part of the architecture. I learned the tech content taught in later Pillars, then reverse-engineered the competencies students needed by the time they arrived there.

That meant taking some learners from “I have never used a smartphone” to building their own websites in Figma and learning basic code within a single full-time quarter.

This wasn’t accidental. It was the product of an intentional, coherent system designed to build capacity, confidence, and stability at scale.

Putting It All Together

Everything was sequenced so:

  • each component built on the last
  • each skill connected to a real, higher-level outcome
  • each day had structure, pacing, and emotional logic
  • instructors had what they needed to facilitate sustainably
  • learners had what they needed to grow without breaking

The result wasn’t just a 12-week program. It was a daily instructional ecosystem, built to hold people through real transformation on a real schedule with real stakes.

4.5 Backward Design in Action: Preparing Learners for the Reality of Tech Careers

The ultimate goal of the program was clear: by the end of Pillar 3, learners needed to secure and sustain a job in the tech industry.

Not just get a job — function within it, navigate its demands, withstand its pressure, and maintain it long-term after a lifetime of incarceration, instability, or interrupted education.

Pillar 3 (Job Search + Career Stability): What Learners Needed

To survive the brutality of the tech job search — and later, the realities of a tech work environment — learners needed:

  • emotional stability
  • communication clarity
  • consistent follow-through
  • confidence under rejection
  • the ability to self-regulate under pressure
  • resilience after years of institutionalization and trauma

These aren’t “nice to have” skills. They are survival skills.

Pillar 2 (UX Design + Technical Fluency): What Learners Needed

Before they even touched UX tools, learners needed:

  • foundational digital literacy
  • file management fluency
  • comfort navigating multiple platforms
  • the stamina to learn complex concepts full-time
  • the ability to troubleshoot independently
  • the confidence to experiment, fail, and try again

Otherwise, the entire UX sequence would collapse under cognitive overload. The tech instruction SMEs were brilliant — but their content assumed a level of digital fluency many learners simply did not have.

What Pillar 1 HAD to Do

To make Pillars 2 and 3 possible, Pillar 1 needed to produce learners who were:

  • digitally competent enough to enter a UX design bootcamp environment
  • emotionally aware enough to withstand the intensity of technical learning
  • resilient enough to face the job search without collapsing
  • regulated enough to survive the transition into full-time employment
  • confident enough to see themselves as someone who belongs in tech

This meant Pillar 1 was not “introductory.” It was foundational, structural, and non-negotiable for the success of the entire year-long program.

Every digital literacy lab, mindset exercise, leadership session, communication task, and reflective practice was designed with these end goals in mind.

Pillar 1 wasn’t just “first.” It was the launchpad — emotionally, cognitively, and technically.

The Result

By reverse-engineering the competencies of Pillars 2 and 3 into Pillar 1, we were able to take learners who entered the program with:

  • no digital skills
  • no experience with professional writing
  • no emotional scaffolding
  • no familiarity with tech tools
  • no internal or external stability

…and prepare them for:

  • full-time UX training
  • intensive job search preparation
  • eventually, life-changing work in the tech industry

This is Backward Design not as a classroom strategy, but as an architectural backbone for human transformation.

5. SME Collaboration: Instructional Designer as Interpreter, Editor, Therapist, Air-Traffic Controller

CROP’s instructors and leaders had incredible content expertise — but not instructional architecture, and definitely not for a full-time learning environment that required daily pacing, emotional containment, and cross-Pillar alignment.

My role expanded quickly into something much larger than “ID support.” It became:

  • pulling ideas out of their heads
  • translating jargon into teachable steps
  • cutting content that didn’t align to outcomes
  • running Backward Design sessions to define competencies for all Pillars
  • reverse-engineering Pillar 1 to prepare learners for the tech intensity of Pillar 2
  • creating templates for lessons, assessments, and instructor scripts
  • coaching SMEs through scope creep and emotional overload
  • ensuring coherence across wildly different teaching styles
  • teaching instructors how to coach learners through heavy leadership content without flooding them

Pillar 2 (Tech Instruction)

The SMEs were brilliant — deeply skilled in UX, coding, and digital design. What they needed wasn’t “fixing”; they needed curricular architecture.

I helped them:

  • map their expert knowledge to teachable sequences
  • integrate their content with the outcomes of Pillar 1
  • define daily pacing that matched a full-time learning environment
  • maintain rigor without overwhelming learners still stabilizing

Pillar 3 (Professional Development)

This Pillar needed something different: real instructional design rescue. The instructor had strong intentions but no idea how to build or teach a structured curriculum.

We reworked her entire Pillar together, including:

  • creating learning outcomes
  • sequencing content
  • writing assessments
  • designing learner-centered activities
  • building her confidence as a facilitator

Coaching SMEs to Coach Learners

Because the leadership content was emotionally loaded and practiced daily, I also supported SMEs in:

  • facilitating vulnerable work without retraumatizing learners
  • pacing heavy content inside a full-time schedule
  • maintaining emotional safety while still challenging learners
  • responding when learners began practicing agency in messy, real ways

This was instructional design as diplomacy. And also project management. And also therapy. I am not a therapist. But I held us all together.

6. Design Frameworks I Used Without Calling Them That

I drew on:

  • ADDIE for overall structure
  • Backward Design for outcomes mapping
  • UDL for accessibility and choice
  • Trauma-informed pedagogy for emotional safety
  • Mayer’s Principles for cognitive load
  • Adult Learning Theory for relevance and autonomy

If you wanted the fancy version, I could rewrite this like an academic citation list. Back then, when I started using these principles, I didn't even know what they were called.

But the truth is: I built something that actually works for real people. That's what matters.

7. Development Highlights: The Actual Making of the Thing

This included creating:

  • a full 12-week, full-time instructional sequence (40 hours/week)
  • daily facilitator scripts with pacing, timing, trauma-informed notes
  • slide decks for lessons, labs, workshops, and leadership circles
  • learner handouts, practice tasks, and activity flows
  • troubleshooting plans for both tech failures and emotional dysregulation
  • performance + formative assessments aligned to cross-Pillar outcomes
  • learner reflection tools designed to build emotional awareness and agency
  • cross-lesson scaffolding to maintain coherence across domains
  • integration points with Pillar 2 (UX design) and Pillar 3 (career readiness)
  • alignment documents, rubrics, and program-wide templates
  • asynchronous modules for reinforcement and differentiated pacing
  • analytics tools to track progress and support instructors
  • badge-based microcredentials to make daily growth visible

All of this had to function inside a daily, full-time learning environment where emotional safety, cognitive load, and stamina mattered as much as content.

This is the point in the story where most IDs would say, “And that was a lot.”

But honestly? I loved it.

There is nothing quite like designing forty hours of learning a week to teach you what really matters in instructional design.

8. Challenges and How I Solved Them (The Fun Part)

Challenge: “We want to teach EVERYTHING.”
Solution: I built outcome-aligned boundaries so “everything” became the right things, at the right time, for the right reasons.

Challenge: Learners arrived with wildly different skill levels — from “never used a smartphone” to “already tech-curious.”
Solution: I designed modular activities, layered scaffolds, differentiation pathways, and daily instructor notes that allowed everyone to move while still feeling successful.

Challenge: The program was full-time (40 hours/week). Learners needed growth without burnout.
Solution: I structured each day with cognitive pacing, emotional logic, and trauma-informed flow: challenge → support → practice → recovery → repeat.

Challenge: Leadership content was emotionally heavy and practiced daily.
Solution: I coached instructors on how to coach learners — how to facilitate vulnerability, regulate group energy, and build agency without sending anyone into overwhelm.

Challenge: Pillar 2 SMEs were brilliant but needed coherence with the rest of the program.
Solution: I used Backward Design to align Pillar 1 with their UX competencies and built structural bridges into their curriculum.

Challenge: Pillar 3 needed a complete instructional rebuild.
Solution: I partnered with the instructor to redesign her entire Pillar — outcomes, sequencing, activities, assessments, everything — so it could actually prepare learners for the job search.

Challenge: Instructor confidence varied wildly.
Solution: I created scripts, flexible pacing guides, troubleshooting notes, and built-in reflection tools so every instructor could facilitate skillfully, regardless of background.

Challenge: No pre-existing materials.
Solution: I made the materials. All of them.

Challenge: The whole program needed to function as a coherent, emotionally safe, multi-Pillar ecosystem.
Solution: I designed Pillar 1 as the backbone — building the emotional, technical, and cognitive stability required for learners to survive and thrive in Pillars 2 and 3.

9. A Note on Impact: Stan

Some outcomes can’t be captured in metrics, but they live vividly in the people who moved through the program. Stan was one of them.

When he entered Pillar 1, his digital literacy was in the negatives. He was a kind, gentle man — the sort of person who should have been retired on a beach in Hawaii — but instead had spent most of his life behind bars. Starting from scratch would have been overwhelming for many people.

Stan didn’t flinch.

He showed up every day ready to learn, ready to try, ready to struggle, ready to try again. He embraced every challenge built into the program — not because it was easy, but because he refused to be anything other than fully alive in the chance he’d been given.

By mid-Pillar 3, he pulled me aside with a quiet, glowing pride and showed me an app he’d built on his phone. The joy on his face — that moment of I did this — is the reason this kind of instructional design work matters.

Before the end of Pillar 4, he landed a job. He moved into an apartment with another learner from his cohort. He started building a life that reflected the person he had always been, long before the system allowed him to show it.

Stan passed away recently, on the ferry between San Francisco and the East Bay — his favorite place in the world. A couple of months before he died, he texted me to say he had started setting reminders on his phone to reach out to the people he cared about. I was one of those people.

He thanked me for being a mentor. But the truth is: the honor was mine.

Stan’s story is not an outlier — it is an example of what becomes possible when learners are met with systems built for their realities, their histories, their strengths, and their futures. He is one of the clearest reasons I do this work.

10. Reflection: What I Learned + What Came Next

This project taught me:

  • that I can build full instructional ecosystems from scratch — not just courses
  • how to lead SMEs with clarity, kindness, and backbone
  • how to design for adults whose skills, histories, and emotional worlds vary dramatically
  • how to build emotional scaffolding into cognitive tasks in a full-time learning environment
  • how to hold a classroom safely through vulnerability, identity shifts, and accelerated growth
  • how to balance structure with flexibility on a daily, hourly, moment-to-moment level
  • how to translate a dream into a system — and a system into real human transformation

And the truth is: I didn’t walk away from this thinking about what I would do next time. I walked away knowing what I am capable of doing now.

I expanded the program as it grew — building analytics, badge systems, scenario practice, instructor tools, alignment documents, and asynchronous modules — not because they were “nice additions,” but because the program needed them to function as the ecosystem it became.

The next time I build something like this (and there will be a next time), I bring all of that with me: the architecture, the emotional logic, the pacing instincts, the systems thinking, the confidence, and the deep respect for what adult learners can accomplish when the design actually supports them.

This project didn’t just teach me what instructional design is. It taught me what kind of instructional designer I am.

And now, I'm ready to bring that same approach — strategic, human-centered, and unafraid of complexity — to the next challenge.
